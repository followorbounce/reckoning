Mirrors

A mirror has one rule: the ray leaves at the same angle to the normal as it arrived. Curve the mirror and the normals tip, and parallel light gathers to a focus. A sphere gathers it almost to a point. A parabola gathers it exactly, but only for light along its axis.

Spherical mirrors

For rays close to the axis, a spherical mirror of radius R focuses parallel light halfway between itself and its centre of curvature C:

A ray striking at height h, where the radius from C makes the angle α with the axis (sin α = h/R), crosses the axis a distance R/(2 cos α) from C. That is nearer the mirror than F, so a wide spherical mirror has spherical aberration like a lens. A convex mirror spreads light as if from a virtual focus behind it. That gives a wide view in a small mirror, at the price of images that look further away than they are.

The parabola

A parabola is the curve whose every point is as far from the focus as from a fixed line behind it. That makes every ray parallel to the axis reflect through the focus, however far from the axis it strikes. Choose the parabolic mirror: the spherical aberration reads zero. Then tilt the light a few degrees. The rays no longer meet at one point but cross unevenly, off to one side. Seen in a real telescope, where the cross-section becomes a whole image, that error is a comet-shaped blur called coma, and it limits the useful field of a parabolic telescope. Used the other way round, a lamp at the focus of a parabola sends out a parallel beam, as in a searchlight. A dish aerial gathers radio waves the same way.

Then and now

Isaac Newton’s first reflecting telescope (1668) used a spherical mirror of polished speculum metal. John Hadley showed the Royal Society a reflector with a true parabolic mirror in 1721, and parabolic main mirrors served most large telescopes until the mid-20th century, the 5 m Hale telescope (1948) among them. Most large telescopes since, from Hubble to Keck, use a pair of hyperbolic mirrors (the Ritchey–Chrétien design) that cancels coma as well. A mirror cannot have chromatic aberration, because reflection treats every colour the same. That is one reason the largest telescopes are reflectors.

What this model leaves out

These are rays, the geometrical-optics picture: light is treated as lines that bend and split at surfaces. Diffraction and interference, which need the wave picture, are not shown. The bench is a flat cross-section, so a lens here is really a cylinder of glass seen end-on. That is exact for rays in the plane of the drawing. Air is taken as n = 1, and the glass and water indices are given relative to air, so the angles are right. Colours on screen are an approximation of the spectrum.
