The double helix

The two strands wind round a common axis with the base pairs stacked between them like the treads of a spiral stair. The same chemistry can take more than one shape: the familiar B form, the squat A form, and the left-handed Z form.

A and B: fibre models of Arnott & Hukins 1972; Z: the crystal structure of Wang et al. 1979. Diameters and tilts are rounded, as given in Saenger 1984. In solution B-DNA turns a little less tightly than in fibres: Wang 1979 measured 10.4 ± 0.1 bp per turn. The build checks that the twist is 360° divided by the base pairs per turn, and that the pitch is the rise times the base pairs per turn. The 3D model is drawn from these numbers. It is a diagram, not an atomic model.

Then and now

The 1953 model had 10 base pairs per turn and a pitch of 3.4 nm, read from Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling’s X-ray photographs of the B form. The left-handed Z form was not seen until 1979, when a short (CG)3 crystal was solved at atomic resolution. In it the backbone zigzags because the repeating unit is two base pairs, not one.
