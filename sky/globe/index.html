Latitude and longitude

Latitude is how far north or south you are, measured as an angle from the equator; longitude is how far east or west, measured from the meridian through Greenwich. Two angles fix any point on Earth. Turn the globe, and tap anywhere to read its position.

How long is a minute?

The Earth is slightly flattened (WGS 84: equatorial radius 6 378 137 m, flattening 1/298.257 223 563), so a minute of latitude is a little longer near the poles, where the surface is flatter. Averaged over the globe it is close to 1852 m, and that is where the nautical mile comes from. A minute of longitude shrinks with the cosine of the latitude and vanishes at the poles.

Topology lens

No flat map can show a sphere without stretching it: Gauss proved in 1827 that distances on a curved surface cannot all be kept on a plane. Every chart therefore chooses what to keep true. Mercator keeps angles, so a constant course is a straight line; the gnomonic projection keeps great circles straight. See both on the sailing page.

Then and now

The British Admiralty mile was 6080 ft (1853.184 m), close to a minute of latitude in the English Channel. The international nautical mile was fixed at exactly 1852 m in 1929; see length units. Latitudes today refer to WGS 84; old charts used local datums that can differ by hundreds of metres.
