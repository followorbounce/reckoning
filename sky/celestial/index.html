The celestial sphere

Seen from Earth, the stars sit on a vast sphere turning once a day. Extend the Earth’s equator and poles outward and you get the sky’s coordinates: declination is latitude on the sky, right ascension is longitude, counted east in hours from the point where the Sun crosses the equator in March. The Sun’s own yearly path, the ecliptic, is tilted 23.4° to that equator.

Signs and constellations

Two thousand years ago the ecliptic was divided into twelve equal signs of 30°, each named after the constellation that lay in it. The equinox, the starting point, slides westward along the ecliptic by about 50.3″ a year, a full circle in about 25 800 years, because the Earth’s axis wobbles like a top (precession). The signs moved with the equinox; the stars did not. Today the March equinox lies in Pisces, and the Sun spends most of each sign in the constellation before it. Since 1930 the constellations also have official boundaries, and the ecliptic crosses thirteen of them, including Ophiuchus.

Then and now

Reckoning treats the zodiac as astronomy: the signs are a coordinate system, 30° steps of ecliptic longitude from the equinox, and that is how they appear in old almanacs and navigation tables. They carry no meaning beyond position.
