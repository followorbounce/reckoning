Kinds of time

“Noon” used to mean the moment the Sun crossed your meridian. That moment drifts through the year, differs from town to town, and is not the moment a star returns to the same place. Each of those facts gave navigators and astronomers a different clock.

The equation of time

A sundial and a clock agree on only four days a year. The difference, the equation of time, comes from two effects: the Earth moves faster near perihelion in early January, and the Sun’s path is tilted to the equator, so equal steps along it are not equal steps of right ascension.

Plot the Sun’s declination against the equation of time and you get the analemma, the figure eight traced by the Sun if you photograph it at the same clock time every few days for a year. Its top and bottom are the solstices; its width is the equation of time.

Time zones at sea

Ships keep zone time: the ocean is divided into 15° strips centred on multiples of 15°, each one hour from the next. The zone description is the number of hours you add to zone time to get UTC, so it is negative east of Greenwich. Each zone also has a letter; J is not used, and Z, for Greenwich, gave “Zulu time”.

Then and now

Until 1925 astronomers began the day at noon, so that a night’s observations kept one date. Greenwich Mean Time was the world’s reference until UTC took over; leap seconds, added since 1972, are to stop by 2035 (CGPM 2022, Res. 4), when UTC will be allowed to drift further from the Earth’s rotation.
